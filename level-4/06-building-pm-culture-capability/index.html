    06 · Building PM Culture & Capability¶

    Templates and gates (Level 3, module 05) standardise what gets produced. They don't produce good project managers. Building capability across a PM organisation — so that quality doesn't depend on which three senior PMs happen to be assigned to the important projects — is a distinct leadership responsibility, and it's usually the first thing cut when budgets tighten, which is exactly backwards.

    A PM competency framework¶

    Competency Level 1 (Associate) Level 2 (PM) Level 3 (Senior PM) Level 4 (Program/Portfolio)
    Planning Follows a template with guidance Builds a plan independently Builds plans others learn from Sequences plans across programs
    Risk management Logs risks when told to Runs qualitative risk process unprompted Runs quantitative EMV analysis Aggregates risk across a portfolio
    Stakeholder management Executes assigned communication Manages own stakeholder map Manages resistance and coalitions Manages board/exec relationships
    Crisis response Escalates immediately Contains with support Leads incident command Sets organisational crisis protocol
    Judgment under ambiguity Needs explicit instruction Makes reasonable calls, checks big ones Makes calls others would've escalated Sets the standard for what needs escalating

    This grid is useful for two things a generic "years of experience" ladder can't do: it lets a PM see specifically what separates their current level from the next one, and it lets an organisation identify a genuine gap (e.g., "we have plenty of Level 2 planners but nobody who can run quantitative risk") rather than a vague sense that "we need more senior people."

    Building capability, not just hiring it¶

    Method What it builds Cost Speed
    Hiring senior PMs externally Immediate capacity at the top level High (comp, ramp-up) Fast for that one hire, doesn't scale the org
    Structured mentoring (senior paired with associate on real projects) Judgment, the hardest thing to teach from a template Low direct cost, real time cost from the senior PM Slow but compounds
    Formal training (courses, certifications) Baseline knowledge (terminology, frameworks) Moderate Fast for knowledge, doesn't build judgment alone
    Communities of practice (PMs sharing real cases, not theory) Pattern recognition across projects Low Slow, requires genuine participation, not mandated attendance
    Rotational assignments across project types Breadth — a PM who's only run Waterfall projects can't lead an Agile-heavy program Moderate (disruption during transition) Slow

    The mistake most organisations make is funding only the top two rows (hiring and formal training) because they're the easiest to procure and report on, while judgment — the competency that actually separates a Level 2 from a Level 3 PM in the framework above — comes almost entirely from mentoring and communities of practice, the two hardest to measure and easiest to defund.

    Worked example: capability gap analysis¶

    A PMO runs a skills audit across 20 PMs against the competency framework:

    Competency # at Level 1 # at Level 2 # at Level 3 # at Level 4
    Planning 3 12 4 1
    Risk management 5 11 3 1
    Crisis response 8 9 2 1
    Stakeholder management 4 10 5 1

    Crisis response is the visible gap: 8 of 20 PMs (40%) are still at Level 1 — meaning if a crisis hits a project run by one of those 8, the organisation's default response (per module 04) is "escalate immediately" rather than any capacity to contain locally. Compare this to planning, where only 3 of 20 (15%) are at Level 1. The capability investment this data justifies is a targeted crisis-response training and shadowing program — not a generic "leadership training for all PMs" initiative that would spread the same budget across a competency where the organisation is already reasonably strong.

    Career pathing tied to the framework¶

    Level Typical project scope Path forward
    Associate Small, well-defined, closely supervised Own progressively larger projects with senior PM shadowing
    PM Full ownership of a mid-size project Take on a project with genuine ambiguity or a difficult stakeholder
    Senior PM Complex or high-risk projects, mentors others Move to program management (Level 3, module 01) or specialise (recovery, transformation)
    Program/Portfolio Cross-project, cross-BU Director track (module 09 covers this transition in depth)

    Making this path explicit and visible — not just implied — is itself a retention tool: PMs who can't see a next level in a legible framework leave for organisations that offer one, independent of how good the actual work is.

    Exercise¶

    Your organisation's skills audit shows 25 PMs: 18 at Level 1–2 on "stakeholder management," 5 at Level 3, 2 at Level 4. Meanwhile, three recent post-incident reviews (module 04) all cite "resistance from a stakeholder coalition that wasn't managed early" as a contributing root cause.

    1. Using the gap-analysis logic from the worked example, state what this data specifically justifies investing in, and what it argues against funding first.
    2. Design a mentoring pairing (which level pairs with which, on what kind of real project) that would target this specific gap.
    3. Using the competency framework's four levels, write one sentence describing what would have to be true of a PM's judgment — not just their knowledge — to move them from Level 2 to Level 3 on stakeholder management specifically.
