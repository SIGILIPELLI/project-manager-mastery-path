    09 · Career Growth: PM to Program/Portfolio Director¶

    The jump from Senior Project Manager to Program or Portfolio Director is not a bigger version of the same job — it's a different job that happens to sit above the old one on an org chart. The skills that made someone an excellent senior PM (detailed planning, hands-on risk management, being the person who personally knows every dependency) can actively work against them at director level, where the job is setting direction and judgment for people doing that hands-on work, not doing it yourself.

    What actually changes¶

    Dimension Senior Project Manager Program/Portfolio Director
    Primary tool The plan, the risk register, direct execution People, priorities, capital allocation decisions
    Success measure This project delivers The portfolio/program delivers its aggregate benefit (Level 3, module 01–02)
    Time horizon Weeks to a year Multiple years, benefit realisation curves (Level 4, module 03)
    Where judgment is exercised On this project's specific risks and trade-offs On which PMs to trust with autonomy, and when to intervene
    Biggest risk of over-indexing on old strengths Micromanaging PMs the way you used to manage your own tasks Losing the ability to spot a real crisis early because you're no longer close to the detail

    The over-indexing trap, worked example¶

    A newly promoted Director, previously an excellent hands-on Senior PM, starts personally reviewing every project's risk register weekly — the habit that made them a great PM.

    Effect Why it happens What it should be replaced with
    PMs stop owning their own registers fully They learn the Director will catch anything they miss A defined escalation threshold (Level 4, module 05) — PMs own their register; only exceptions surface to the Director
    Director has no time for the actual director-level work (portfolio sequencing, capital allocation) All their time is consumed at the project level, the level they should have delegated Time explicitly blocked for portfolio-level work, protected the same way project time used to be protected
    PMs under this Director develop slower They're never given real ownership+consequence, the mechanism that builds judgment (module 06) Genuine delegation, with the Director available for input, not doing the work by proxy

    The habits that earned the promotion are frequently the exact habits that, left unchanged, make someone a bad director — this is worth saying plainly in a mentoring conversation, because it isn't intuitive to someone who was just rewarded for those habits.

    A self-assessment for the transition¶

    Question Senior PM answer Director answer needed
    "How do you know a project is in trouble?" "I review the risk register and EVM myself weekly" "The PM escalates per agreed thresholds, and I trust the escalation because I've built a relationship and a track record with them"
    "How do you resolve a resource conflict?" "I negotiate directly with the other PM" "I apply the portfolio's priority model (module 02) and communicate the trade-off, even when it disappoints someone I like"
    "What's your daily focus?" "Today's blockers on my project" "Which of my 6 program/portfolio-level decisions this quarter are at risk of slipping"
    "How do you develop your team?" "I show them how I'd do it" "I let them make a call I'd have made differently, if the downside is recoverable, because that's how judgment is built (module 06)"

    Building the case for promotion¶

    Evidence type Weak (still PM-level) Strong (director-ready)
    Delivery track record "I delivered 5 projects on time" "I sequenced 3 related projects across a shared resource constraint and hit the program's benefit target (module 01 pattern)"
    Risk handling "I kept my risk register current" "I identified a correlated risk across two projects that neither PM alone would have seen (module 03 pattern)"
    People development "My team liked working for me" "Two people I mentored are now running their own projects independently, including one who handled a real crisis without escalating to me first (module 04, module 06)"
    Executive interaction "I presented my project's status" "I built the ask/options structure that got a $200,000 reserve approved in one meeting (module 05 pattern)"

    The distinguishing feature in every "strong" row is the same: evidence of operating and creating value above the single-project level, not just excelling within it — that's the actual gap a promotion committee is checking for, whether or not they phrase it that way.

    Exercise¶

    You are a Senior PM being considered for a Program Director role. In your current job you personally resolved a resource conflict between two other PMs last month by negotiating directly with both of them.

    1. Using the self-assessment table's format, write the "Senior PM answer" you actually gave, and the "Director answer" a promotion committee would want to see instead for the same question about resolving resource conflicts.
    2. Identify one habit from your (hypothetical) current PM role that would become an over-indexing trap if unchanged at Director level, using the worked example's structure (effect / why it happens / replacement).
    3. Write one piece of "strong" evidence (per the promotion-case table) you could plausibly build toward in the next two quarters, starting from where you are now.
